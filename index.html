The sky project, 25 March to 10 August 2020

Covid Sky Lab

Twenty-seven people photographed the same patch of sky from home every day of India's 2020 lockdown, at a minute each of them chose. These are ways of reading what they kept. Every swatch, strip, note and thumbnail comes from the photographs. Each approach is its own page.

1

The attendance sheet

One row per person, ordered by the clock time they chose, one swatch per day. The swatch is the mean colour of the top third of that day's photo. Gaps are left as gaps. Hover a cell to see the frame.

order by

Draws on On Kawara's I Got Up and Holland Hopson's Average Color of the Sky. Build as a long horizontal print (139 columns) or a scrolling web page; the row order by clock time is what makes it more than a calendar.

2

The grid timelapse

Every person is a cell. Press play and the archive runs at one day per frame. A cell is dim before its person starts, hatched on a day they missed, and fades once they stop. Watch how many are left in August.

Draws on Tehching Hsieh's Time Clock Piece. Build as a single video for a wall, with one click of sound per photo taken; the sound thins out as the grid empties.

3

The averaged sky

Two averages. First, every frame a person shot, stacked and averaged: their view as a portrait, with the sky as a blur of all their days. Second, the sky bands of everyone who shot on a given day, averaged into one strip: the country's late-afternoon sky, day by day.

One sky per day, averaged across everyone who shot (days with three or more people)

Draws on Jason Salavon's amalgamations and Penelope Umbrico's Suns. Build as a print set of 26 portraits captioned name, area, city, time, days; the day strip as a long ribbon print.

4

The frame as fingerprint

Pixels that barely changed across a person's run are their fixed frame: a roof edge, a tower, a tank, a tree. Everything that changed is the sky. Here the sky is removed and replaced by the swatch for the chosen day, so the same roof appears again and again under a different colour.

Draws on Byron Kim's Sunday Paintings and Sugimoto's Seascapes. The mask is computed from pixel variance, so it is only as good as the person's steadiness; it works best for the long, steady runs. Build as a set of silkscreens, one plate per person, the sky printed as a flat colour that changes per edition.

5

The 4 pm choir

A single day read down the clock. Each person sits at the minute they chose, from Ishan before noon to Rizwan at six. Move the day and the skies change while the positions stay fixed. People who did not shoot that day fall silent. Press Listen and each day is played as a sweep down the clock: one beep per person at their minute, low before noon and rising to six, or flat if you bring the pitch range down to nothing. People are grouped in half-hour bands so everyone fits on one screen; the sound still uses their exact minute.

Draws on Jan Dibbets' Shortest Day and the Reuters Delhi window. Build as a vertical web piece or a tall print, one per day, with the clock as the spine.

6

Ribbons

Each person is a ribbon hung from a rod. Where it hangs from is their clock time. Its length is the number of days they showed up. Each slice is the actual sky band from that day's photo; a missed day is a thread.

Draws on Stefanie Posavec's Air Transformed. Build as dye-sublimation prints on fabric, hung from a single rod at heights set by clock time; the longest ribbon (Samar, 127 days) would reach the floor.

7

A map that fills and empties

Each city is a point coloured by the average sky of everyone who shot there that day. A city with nobody shooting goes hollow. Scrub the days and watch the map thin out from the south upward.

Draws on your own 2020 note about map navigation, and Eri King's Collective Sky. The outline is a rough hand-drawn polygon, to be replaced with proper geometry. Rizwan and Saranya have no city on the sheet and are not placed.

8

What the sky said, what the instruments said

Delhi, Ghaziabad and Rewari together: a daily blueness index computed from the photographs, plotted against the official daily air-quality record. The photograph line is real. The instrument line is an empty slot because no archived 2020 station data was reachable from here; the CPCB daily bulletins for those dates would fill it. Two press anchors are marked.

Sky blueness from photos (Delhi NCR, daily mean)CPCB daily AQI, to be filled

Blueness is the blue channel minus the red channel of the sky swatch, so haze and cloud pull it down and clear blue pushes it up. Anchors: NPR reported Delhi's AQI around 93 on 10 April 2020 against a March 2019 average of 161; NASA reported aerosol levels over north India at a 20-year low in the week of 5 April. Draws on Zou Yi, who posts each photo with the day's AQI.

9

Sky tunes

Every sky swatch sits somewhere on one continuum, from hazy white through grey to clear blue, and that continuum is a pitch. A person's run becomes their tune: one note per day, silence where they missed. Pick people to play together and change the selection while it runs. A missed day does not go silent: the last note is held and fades a little each day until the person returns, or dies away if they don't.

The scale: real swatches from the archive, ordered by blueness, with the note each one plays

Blueness is the blue channel minus the red channel of the sky swatch, centred on G4. The pitch range slider sets how far blueness spreads the notes, from none (every day the same note, only the rhythm of showing up remains) to four octaves. With snap on, notes land on a pentatonic scale so overlapping people stay consonant; off, the pitch is continuous. Each person is panned left or right by their city's longitude, so Bombay sits left and Shillong right. Click a row to add or remove that person.

10

Same clock, different sun

The first location experiment. Five o'clock in Shillong is not five o'clock in Bombay: the sun is lower in the east and the sky already warming. Here each photo is placed by the sun's elevation at the moment it was taken, computed from the date, the clock time and the city's coordinates, instead of by date. Much of the colour difference between people is the sun, not the air.

Elevation is the sun's angle above the horizon in degrees, from a standard solar-position formula using Indian Standard Time and each city's latitude and longitude; accuracy is about a degree. People with no city on the sheet are left out. Next location experiments this opens up: order the sheet by latitude (already on prototype 1), mark each city's 2020 monsoon onset on its row, compare people in the same city on the same day, and pan or place sound by longitude.

11

Each person's sky

No counts, no ranking. Every run fills the same width, so a week and four months sit as equals, and what you see is the colour of the sky that person kept. Ordered from the bluest sky to the warmest. Run your mouse along a band to lift that day out with the lens. The line under each name is written from the run itself: what its sky was mostly like, its bluest and greyest days, and the habit of the person behind it.

Draws on Byron Kim's Sunday Paintings and Sugimoto's Seascapes, where the frame is fixed and only the weather and light speak. Missed days are the thin dark seams. Time-of-day habit is measured against the minute each person chose.

12

The day it was

One person, one photograph at full size, and what that day was in the country under the sky. Step through with the arrows or the keyboard. Under each frame: the new cases reported that day in the person's state and, from late April, their district; the lockdown phase; and one line from the day's national record. The local headline is the Times of India city page for that day, recovered from the Internet Archive.

Cases are daily new confirmed counts from the covid19india.org archive, by state, and by district from 26 April 2020 when district reporting begins. The line of the day is the opening sentence of that date's entry in the Wikipedia timeline of the pandemic in India, unedited, so its register varies; it is a placeholder for a chosen headline. Local headlines come from the Times of India city section for each city (Gurgaon stands in for Rewari, Coimbatore for Erode, Kozhikode for Calicut), as archived by the Internet Archive on that date. The main line is the day's story whose title mentions the virus or the lockdown; the small lines are the other stories archived that day. Each links to the archived page. Frames here are 640 by 480 crops of the originals.

13

Your sky, played back

The archive returned to the person who made it. One tune brought to the fore: their run, one note per day, missed days held and fading. Play it through, slow it down, change the instrument, or just run your hand along the sky and hear each day as you pass over it. This is the page each participant would get, six years on.

Play is the way in. Hovering plays the day under the cursor, so a person can find their bluest day by ear. The band is their run at full width; the thin dark seams are the days they missed, and you hear those as the note fading. As a gift this would be a page per person with a link sent to them, cover image and all.

14

What moved

Below the sky, in the part of the frame that was supposed to stay still, something usually didn't. For each long run, the frame's textured region is searched for the two places that changed most across the days, and those patches are laid out in order. A car that left, laundry, a tree filling in, a floor added to a building. Small proofs that life carried on.

Method. Frames are averaged; the sky is excluded by keeping only textured regions of the average; the day-to-day variance of what remains is smoothed and its two strongest peaks become the patches, 112 pixels square on the 640-wide frame. Runs shorter than 15 days are skipped. A hand-held phone never returns to exactly the same spot, so some patches show the frame shifting rather than the world changing; those are honest too. Hover a patch for its date.

15

The air at that hour

The question the project began with, asked properly for the first time. For every photo, the official air-quality reading at the hour it was taken, from the monitoring station or city network nearest to the person. Above each row, the sky the person saw; below it, the air the instruments measured. Then all of it on one chart: how blue the sky looked against how clean the air was.

Every photo with a reading: sky blueness against AQI at that hour

16

Cleanest air, dirtiest air

For each person, two photographs from their own run: the day the instruments read cleanest and the day they read worst. Nothing else. If the air is visible in the sky, it should be visible here, in the pair.

Readings are the AQI at the hour of each photo, as on the previous page, so the same coverage limits apply and people with no reading are not shown. Blueness is printed under each frame so you can check the sky against the number without trusting your eye. Frames are 640 by 480 crops of the originals.
